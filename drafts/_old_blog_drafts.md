## My whisky journey - 2024-04-21

I tried my first glass of whisk(e)y at a Swedish club, 'Soft Bar', mostly attended by newly-eighteens such as myself back in 2012. "This is amazing!" I thought, having paid 120kr (~12€) for what was probably a Jack Daniels on the rocks. I felt sophisticated sitting at a table sippin' my whiskey, watching the other animals embarrass themselves on the dance floor.

A year later I bought my first bottle of whisky at Nice airport: a Bowmore Enigma, which lured me in with a reddish glow (that I now know is due to caramel colourant) and a pretty packaging promising a preponderance of peat. At this point I had decided I was a “peat guy” after having tried some whiskies from my university chapter’s quite well-stocked but peat-centred cupboard. Clearly that was the most serious and manly kind of whisky, I thought. Perhaps I bought that whisky bottle in Nice as a feeble attempt to recover some sense of manhood from the embarrassment of having arrived at the airport too late to catch my flight...

Then followed years of not so much whisky, but a whole lot of beer: blind tastings, festivals, beer trips to Belgium with friends, running a University brewing club, and being generally nerdy about beer.

When I moved to Edinburgh in 2017 to start a PhD programme, I decided to put my brewing days behind me. Maybe it was time to get into whisky for real this time? My stint as a “beer snob” had taught me a thing or two about drinking with intention. Stumbling into Cadenhead's whisky shop on the Royal Mile, still rather naïve to whisky beyond familiarity with a few famous brands, I let myself be guided into a glorious new world of smell and taste. I remember that winter in Edinburgh very fondly. I was reading through the works of Tolkien, sipping delicate sherry-matured Glen Grant and Benrinnes of vintages close to my own. I was a changed man: a devotee of the Glory of Speyside.

In 2018 I joined the Water of Life society (WOLS) of the University of Edinburgh. My whisky experiences began multiplying fast: weekly 5-dram tastings and two multi-day distillery trips a year. Each tasting had a theme, building up from flavour profiles, cask types, and so on in the beginning of the academic year, to more niche tastings like ‘Whisky vs new make', and 'Stuff the President bought at auction' at the end of towards the end. The level of knowledge and passion for whisky in the society is truly impressive. Several members I got to know were studying brewing and distilling, or working shifts at the Royal Mile Whiskies shop. Some are today operating whisky distilleries in various places in the world.

During these years in Scotland, as I experienced the full range of Scotch whisky my tastes continually evolved. From the intitial fascination with peat bombs and then sherry bombs, to subtler ex-bourbon and sherry matured whiskies of age. A special 30yo Linkwood that I had been eyeing (and trying) at Cadenhead's became my 25th birthday gift from my father. A landmark bottle, raising Linkwood to favourite distillery for years. With perhaps Bunnahabhain, with its salty caramel notes, a good second.  

With my growing fascination for whisky and past experience running a beer society it was perhaps inevitable that I would eventually end up on the WOLS committee (in the end as treasurer in 19/20). This brought me joy and purpose. And Zoe. In the crowded atmosphere of the society's pub of choice, the Dadga, we fell in love.

Half-way through the term the pandemic broke out. In the UK, we found ourselves in lockdown and our lives utterly disrupted. The society trip to Islay that was booked and ready had to be cancelled, tastings were put on indefinite hold. Whisky shops and pubs closed their doors. Yet, some months into this new reality, the desire for some semblance of normalcy drove us to action. On my initiative, the committee found a way to distribute whisky tasting samples around town and run very appreciated online tastings for many months, until the the lockdowns finally lifted.

By then it had become time for me to leave Edinburgh and start a postdoc in Germany. I had gathered quite a collection of bottles, and the issue of shipping them was not trivial. In the end I chartered a third of of a lorry and hid the bottles among all my other possessions - my first successful whisky smuggling operation!

In the little town in Schleswig-Holstein were my new home lay, I found myself without a whisky society, without a local whisky shop, and without a local pub worth the visit. Luckily there's the internet. It turns out that Germany is a far larger market for Scotch whisky than Scotland, with more competition and lower alcohol tax. In the end, I'm sampling more Scotch whisky here than I ever did in Scotland, much thanks to small independent online whisky shops, and also thanks to the many whisky fairs held around. ## Cognac vs Armagnac

Both are similar in that they are grape distillates from nearby regions (Cognac and Armagnac) in southern France. Cognac is all the more famous, having historically, and to this day, been produced for export. Armagnac the French like to keep for themselves, I guess. It is the oldest tradition of eaux-de-vie in France. Some further points of comparison:

Terroir. The Cognac region generally has chalky terroir, partitioned into six areas or 'crus' (Grande Champagne, Petite Champagne, Borderies, Fins Bois, Bons Bois, Bois Ordinaires); Armagnac has more clay, and three crus (Bas-Armagnac, Tenereze, and the tiny Haute-Armagnac). 

Grapes. Cognac uses almost exclusively Ugni Blanche (97%). In Armagnac it is used 55%, and 35% Baco, with a few others.

Distillation. Cognac is double distilled in pot stilled, similar to whisky. Armagnac is column distilled, but not in such large stills as grain whisky.

Flavour. Cognac is considered more elegant and refined, with a lighter texture; Armagnac is more wild, and viscuous, and is generally higher ABV before dilution. As a whisky enthusiast, it's hard not think Armagnac sounds the more interesting on paper. 

I have only ever tried a handful of Armagnacs and Cognacs. So my taste reference inevitably falls back to a comparison with Scotch.

## Michelin grade

The question on what system to use in scoring whisky has remained with this blog since its beginning. As I wrote in an early post:

Ultimately, a spirit review is attempting the impossible: to capture in static language a multifaceted sensory experience that evolves with time and is shaped by personal memories and associations. As difficult as describing a whisky is even using one’s full powers of language, even more so to reduce it down to a final score in a meaningful way.

The first scoring system (March-May 2023) therefore leaned heavily into multidimensionality. I tried assessing variety, balance, intensity, distinctiveness and a summary dimension of overall enjoyability, with a few levels of distinction for each. Way too much effort! And the system of symbols I used was not very transparent. I then switched to a simple summary mark. After some iterations of the categories I settled on: bad, unfulfilling, rewarding, personal favourite (❤️). But by August 2024 I had concluded this was too boring, as almost all the stuff I review is "rewarding" (with the occasional ❤️) . So I made a new pass at a multidimensional system comprising deliciousness, fun factor, and value for money on a linear but somewhat diffuse scale using stoplight symbols. By August 2025 I had revised it into the current system, where I judge deliciousness and fun factor for the nosing and sipping, separately, using a colour bar: 

Deliciousness
Fun factor

Exceptional value for money is awarded an additional ⭐ and the personal favourite remains a special ❤️ distinction. In principle, I'm quite happy with this system. But going forward I want to make it quicker and easier to review whiskies and to write posts.

So the pendulum is now swinging back to a categorical system (let's see how long it lasts). Some of the philosophy behind the old categories remains: I'm not after a subtle gradations from bad to good to fantastic. I want to highlight what's really worth paying special attention to in an endless sea of good and enjoyable whiskies. (I always never review anything that's bad.) In this regard, I think the Michelin Guide follows a similar idea. 

 1 Star: "A very good restaurant in its category"

 2 Stars: "Excellent cooking, worth a detour"

 3 Stars: "Exceptional cuisine, worth a special journey"

♦ Conditionally noteworthy

A whisky of high quality that stands out clearly within its intended context of price and style.

Buying note: A great buy within its category.

★★ Absolute excellence

A whisky of exceptional quality that stands among the great whiskies, regardless of age, price, or category.

Buying note: Worth paying a premium for, but not necessarily any premium.

★★★ Transcendent greatness

A whisky of profound quality and impact that represents a pinnacle of the whisky experience.

Buying note: Worth a splurge, even if only to experience it once.

FAQ

Q: Is the scoring system consistent across all reviews?

No. The scoring system has changed several times. The current one described above applied from review #430 onwards. The iterations of the scoring system have been described here, here, and here.

Q: Why do most whiskies not have a star?
A whisky without a symbol is not a criticism. Stars are reserved for whiskies of absolute or transcendent excellence. Most whiskies fall into the no-symbol category; they are solid, enjoyable, and competent, but do not command special attention or a premium recommendation.

Q: Can a ★★ whisky also be a great value?
Yes. Stars measure quality, not price. If a ★★ whisky happens to be unusually affordable, this is noted in ░ 1/4
